Score primarily on:
- Correctness of technical claims, not fluency in making them.
- Depth under follow-up: whether a confident first answer survived being pushed on.
- Resume claims: compare what the resume says with what the candidate could explain when
  asked. A claim explained with specifics is strong evidence. A claim they could not explain
  is a negative, however well it reads on paper.
- Code submitted during the round: correctness first, then edge cases, then complexity,
  then readability. Weigh submitted code above spoken description of code.
- Debugging and trade-off reasoning: can they say why, not only what.

Discount:
- Buzzword density and name-dropping of tools with no detail behind it.
- Polish of delivery. A hesitant correct answer beats a smooth wrong one.
- The closing questions the candidate asked at the end. They are not part of the score.

Treat as significant negatives:
- Confidently stated incorrect fundamentals.
- Coding answers that were never made to work and were not reasoned about.
- Contradicting an earlier answer without acknowledging it.

Calibrate to {{experience}}: do not penalise a mid-level candidate for lacking
architecture ownership, and do not reward a senior one for fundamentals alone.

How to score this round, in order of importance:
1. Coding is the strongest evidence. Programming areas are scored only from code the candidate
   actually wrote: whether it is correct on the examples, handles edge cases, has sensible time
   and space complexity, and is readable. Several coding problems of rising difficulty tell more
   than any amount of explanation. If no code was written, the programming areas are not
   assessed: say so, do not guess a score.
2. Technical depth. A correct answer that explains how and why is high; correct but shallow is
   middle; vague, generic or wrong is low. Weigh the hardest question in an area most: a hard
   problem failed is not offset by easy ones passed.
3. Reasoning. Reward a candidate who works a problem out aloud, tests their own answer and
   corrects a mistake. Do not reward a confident answer that is wrong.
4. Coverage. Only score what was tested. An area no question reached is "not assessed", not a
   middling number. The overall score is built from the assessed areas alone.
5. Say what the candidate got right or wrong in each area, specifically, so a recruiter reading
   it can see exactly why the score is what it is and judge whether to hire.
